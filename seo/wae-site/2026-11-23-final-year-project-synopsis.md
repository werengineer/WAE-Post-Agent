## Body

# Final Year Project Synopsis: Format, Sections and Examples for Engineering Students

Your guide wants a synopsis before you start the project. You have an idea, but not a document. This guide shows the sections most colleges ask for, what to write in each one, and the mistakes that get a synopsis sent back.

### The short answer

A final year project synopsis is a short proposal that you submit before you build your project. It says what problem you will solve, how you will solve it, which tools you need, and when you will finish. It is not the full report. Most colleges have their own template, so **your college template always wins** over any general guide, including this one.

### Synopsis vs project report

| | Synopsis | Project report |
|---|---|---|
| When | Before or at the start of the project | After the work is done |
| Purpose | Get approval for your plan | Document what you built and found |
| Content | Plan, method, expected outcome | Method, implementation, results, conclusion |
| Length | Short. Public examples range from about 3 to 10 pages, and colleges differ | Long. Depends on college and branch |

The synopsis talks about the future ("we will build"). The report talks about the past ("we built"). Do not claim results in a synopsis if you have not built anything yet.

### The usual sections of a synopsis

Public college templates and guides differ, but most include the parts below. Use your template's order and names if it has them.

#### Title page and details
Project title, student names, roll numbers, branch, college, and the name of your guide or supervisor. Some templates also ask for a college logo and signatures of the guide and the students.

#### Title
Make it specific. "Smart Home System" is vague. A good title names the thing, the method and the use, for example "Soil-moisture based automatic irrigation controller using a microcontroller". This is an example of the style, not a project to copy.

#### Introduction and problem statement
Say in a few lines what the area is and what is wrong or missing today. Then state the problem in one clear paragraph. A reader should know the problem after reading only this part.

#### Objectives
List what the project will achieve. Keep it to a few points that you can test. One public guide suggests 4 to 6. Too many objectives is a common reason for rejection, because a team cannot finish them.

#### Literature review or existing system
Name two or three existing papers, products or methods, say what they do, and say what they do not do. End with the gap your project fills. Some colleges ask for a minimum number of references, so check your template.

#### Proposed method
Explain how you will build it in steps: the main modules or stages and how they connect. A block diagram or flow chart helps. Software projects can list modules. Mechanical, civil, electrical and electronics projects can list design, analysis, fabrication or simulation, and testing stages.

#### Tools, hardware and materials
List software, programming languages, simulation tools, components, materials and lab facilities. Mark what you already have and what you must arrange. Some templates ask for "facilities available and required".

#### Timeline
Break the work into stages with a start and end week or month. See the table below.

#### Expected outcome and limitations
Say what you expect to have at the end, for example a working prototype, a simulation, a tested model or a software tool. Say honestly what the project will not cover. This shows your guide that you understand the scope.

#### References
List the papers, books, standards and websites you used. Use the style your college asks for. Many engineering reports use IEEE style, but check your template.

### A simple timeline table

This is an example layout. Change the stages and the number of weeks to fit your project and your college calendar.

| Stage | What you do | Example duration |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Study | Read papers, study existing systems, finalise scope | Weeks 1 to 3 |
| 2. Design | Make block diagram, choose tools and parts, plan tests | Weeks 4 to 6 |
| 3. Build | Code, assemble or simulate the first working version | Weeks 7 to 12 |
| 4. Test | Test, find faults, improve, record results | Weeks 13 to 15 |
| 5. Document | Write the report, prepare for the viva | Weeks 16 to 18 |

Use text in your synopsis, not a picture of a table, so your guide can edit it.

### Length and formatting: what to check with your college

Sources disagree here because each college sets its own rules. Public templates ask for different fonts, line spacing and page limits. Examples seen: Times New Roman 12 pt with 1.15 spacing at one college, and 1.5 spacing and one-sided printing at another. Page counts quoted for synopses range from about 3 to 10 pages, and some templates cap single sections, such as the literature survey. Ask your project coordinator for the template before you write.

### Common mistakes to avoid

- **A vague title.** Name the thing and the method.
- **No clear problem statement.** If the problem is unclear, the project is unclear.
- **Too many objectives.** Pick the few you can finish and test.
- **No tools or hardware listed.** Your guide cannot judge if the plan is realistic.
- **No timeline.** Without one, approval is harder.
- **No limitations.** Every project has limits. Say them.
- **Copied content.** Do not copy a senior's synopsis. Use old synopses only to see the layout.
- **Unchecked text from an AI tool.** If you use a tool to help with wording, check every line against what you actually plan to do.
- **Claiming results before building.** Write what you expect, not what you got.

### Checklist before you submit

- [ ] I am using my college template and order.
- [ ] My title names the thing, the method and the use.
- [ ] The problem is stated in one clear paragraph.
- [ ] I have a small number of testable objectives.
- [ ] I named existing work and the gap I fill.
- [ ] I listed tools, hardware and what I still need.
- [ ] My timeline fits my college calendar.
- [ ] I wrote the limitations.
- [ ] My references are in the style my college asks for.
- [ ] Everyone in the team has read the final copy.
- [ ] My guide has seen a draft.

## FAQ

### What is a final year project synopsis?
It is a short proposal submitted before you build your project. It states the problem, objectives, method, tools, timeline and expected outcome so your guide can approve the plan.

### What is the difference between a project synopsis and a project report?
A synopsis is a plan written before the work. A report documents the finished work, with implementation, results and conclusion. The synopsis is much shorter.

### How many pages should a project synopsis be?
There is no single rule. Public examples range from about 3 to 10 pages, and some colleges limit individual sections. Check your department's template.

### What should be in a project synopsis?
Most templates include a title page, title, introduction and problem statement, objectives, literature review or existing system, proposed method, tools and facilities, timeline, expected outcome, limitations and references. Follow your college template.

### How many objectives should a synopsis have?
Keep them few and testable. One public guide suggests 4 to 6. Choose only what your team can finish in the time you have.

### Can I copy a senior's synopsis?
No. Copying is plagiarism. Use old synopses only to see the layout, and write your own content.

### Which citation style should I use in the synopsis?
