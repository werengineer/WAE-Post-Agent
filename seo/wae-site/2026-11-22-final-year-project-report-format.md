## Body

# Final Year Project Report Format for Engineering Students

Your final year project report is the document your examiner reads before the viva. It shows what you built, why you built it, and what you learned. This guide gives the usual structure, what to put in each part, and the mistakes that cost students marks.

**Important:** every college and university has its own template. Margins, fonts, page limits, number of copies and citation style differ. Use this guide to understand the parts. Use your department's template for the exact rules.

## The short answer

Most engineering project reports in India follow the same skeleton:

1. **Front matter:** title page, certificate, acknowledgement, abstract, table of contents, list of figures and tables.
2. **Main chapters:** introduction, literature review, methodology (design), implementation, results and discussion, conclusion and future scope.
3. **Back matter:** references and appendices.

The exact chapter names and order change by college. Some merge methodology and implementation. Some add a chapter on system requirements or testing. If your college gives a template, follow it first.

## The usual order of a project report

| Part | What it does |
|---|---|
| Title page | Project title, your names and roll numbers, guide, department, college, year |
| Certificate and declaration | Signed by your guide and head of department; many colleges also ask for a declaration that the work is your own (TODO: check your college form) |
| Acknowledgement | Short thanks to your guide, staff and team |
| Abstract | A one-paragraph summary of the whole project |
| Table of contents, lists of figures and tables | Let the reader find things fast |
| Chapter 1: Introduction | The problem, why it matters, your objectives |
| Chapter 2: Literature review | What others have done and what is still missing |
| Chapter 3: Methodology / design | How you planned and designed the solution |
| Chapter 4: Implementation | What you built, with diagrams, screenshots, key code |
| Chapter 5: Results and discussion | What worked, test results, what did not |
| Chapter 6: Conclusion and future scope | What you achieved, limits, next steps |
| References | All sources, in one consistent style |
| Appendices | Long code, datasheets, raw data, forms |

## What to write in each part

### Abstract

Write it last, even though it appears first. In one paragraph, say the problem, your approach, your main result and why it matters. Do not add new information that is not in the report. One public guide suggests about 150 to 250 words; check your college limit (TODO: confirm per college).

### Introduction

Say what problem you solved and why it is worth solving. Then list your objectives in clear points. A reader should know after one or two pages what the project is about.

### Literature review

Do not copy summaries of papers one after another. Group related work by theme, say what each approach does well and where it falls short, and end with the gap your project fills. Use credible sources such as journal papers, conference papers, standards, datasheets and official documentation.

### Methodology and implementation

Explain how you designed and built the project, step by step, so another student could repeat it. Include block diagrams, circuit diagrams, flowcharts, architecture diagrams or CAD views as fits your branch. Caption every figure and table and refer to it in the text. Put long code and bulky data in the appendix and show only key parts in the chapter.

### Results and discussion

Show what happened. Use tables, graphs, photos or test outputs. Then explain them: did the project meet your objectives? Compare with earlier work if you can. Be honest about what did not work. Examiners respect a clear explanation of a limit more than a hidden problem.

### Conclusion and future work

Sum up what you achieved against your objectives. State the limits. Suggest realistic next steps. Do not add new results here.

### References and appendices

Pick one citation style your college accepts (for example IEEE) and use it everywhere. Every reference in the list should be cited in the text, and the other way round. Appendices hold long code, datasheets, raw data, survey forms and similar material.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Copying text** from other reports or the web. Write in your own words and cite your sources. Many colleges ask for a signed declaration and some check similarity.
- **No clear problem statement.** If the introduction does not say the problem, the rest feels loose.
- **A literature review that is only a list** of papers with no gap.
- **Screenshots and figures without captions** or without any mention in the text.
- **Results with no discussion.** A graph alone does not explain anything.
- **A report that does not match the demo.** The viva will test this. See our guide to [final year project viva preparation](/blog/final-year-project-viva-preparation).
- **Starting the report in the last week.** Leave time for guide review and corrections.
- **Inconsistent references,** mixed styles, or sources listed but never cited.

## A simple writing plan

1. Get the college template and checklist in week one of the project.
2. Keep a project log: decisions, tests, problems, photos, source links. This becomes your methodology and results.
3. Draft the introduction and literature review early. Update them when the project changes.
4. Save every diagram and screenshot with a clear file name as you work.
5. Write methodology and implementation while the work is fresh.
6. Add results and discussion as soon as you have test data.
7. Write the abstract and conclusion last.
8. Give your guide a full draft in time to correct it. Then proofread the whole document once for spelling, numbering and references.

## FAQ

### What is the format of a final year project report?
Most reports have front matter (title page, certificate, acknowledgement, abstract, contents), main chapters (introduction, literature review, methodology, implementation, results and discussion, conclusion and future scope) and back matter (references, appendices). Your college template decides the exact format.

### How many pages should a final year project report have?
There is no single rule. It depends on your college and branch. Public examples range from about 40 to 100 pages. Check your department's guidelines (TODO: confirm).

### What should be in the abstract of a project report?
The problem, your approach, your main result and why it matters, in one paragraph. Write it after the rest of the report.

### Which chapters are in a project report?
Common chapters are introduction, literature review, methodology or design, implementation, results and discussion, and conclusion with future scope. Some colleges add chapters such as system requirements or testing.

### How do I write the literature review?
Group related work by theme, explain strengths and limits, and end with the gap your project fills. Do not just list papers.

### Can I copy a senior's project report?
No. Copying is plagiarism, and many colleges ask for a declaration that the work is your own or check similarity. Use old reports only to see how a report is laid out, and write your own content.

### Which citation style should I use?
Use the style your college requires. If it does not say, IEEE is common in engineering. Use one style for the whole report.

### When should I start writing the report?
At the start of the project. Keep a log of decisions, tests and sources. Draft chapters as you finish the matching work, and write the abstract and conclusion last.

### What is the difference between a project report and a synopsis?
A synopsis is a short plan written at the start of the project. The report is the full document written at the end. Your college may name or format them differently (TODO: confirm common practice).
