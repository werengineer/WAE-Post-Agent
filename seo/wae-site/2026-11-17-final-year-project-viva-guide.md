## Body

# How to Prepare for Your Final Year Project Viva

## The short answer

To prepare for a final year project viva, make sure you can explain what you built, why you built it that way, how you tested it, and what you personally did. Write a one-page project map, practise the common questions aloud, and test your demo before the day. Examiners usually value clear reasoning over memorised answers.

## What examiners are really checking

A viva is a short conversation where examiners test whether the work is yours and whether you understand it. Most questions fall into four groups:

- **Understanding:** What problem does it solve? Who is it for? Why this approach?
- **Evidence:** How did you test it? What were the results? What went wrong?
- **Ownership:** Which part did you build? What would you explain if asked to open the code?
- **Limits:** What does it not do? What would you improve next?

If you can answer these four groups with specifics, you are most of the way ready.

## Step 1: Confirm the rules with your college

Rules differ by college and university. Before you practise anything, ask your guide or department:

- The viva date, reporting time and what you must carry (report, file, laptop, ID).
- Whether there is a presentation, a live demo, or both.
- How long you get, and whether every team member is asked questions.
- Whether the report must match the final build exactly.

Your department's instructions always come before any general advice, including this guide.

## Step 2: Make a one-page project map

Write one page that matches your final report and your working build. Include:

1. Title and one-line aim
2. The problem and who has it
3. Objectives and scope (what you did and did not cover)
4. Main modules and which one is yours
5. Tools, languages and why you chose them
6. How you tested it, and the results
7. Limitations and future work

This page doubles as your revision sheet. Any place where the report and the build disagree will show up here, and it is better to find it now than in front of the examiners.

## Step 3: Prepare for the questions that come up most

You cannot guess every question, but these themes appear again and again. Prepare a two-sentence answer for each.

**Problem and motivation**
- What problem does your project solve, and for whom?
- Why did you choose this topic?
- What is the scope of your project?

**Design and architecture**
- How is your system structured? Can you draw it?
- Why did you choose this design over another?
- Can you trace one request or one data flow from start to end?

**Tools and technology**
- Why did you choose this language, framework or hardware?
- Explain the module you built. Can you walk through its code or circuit?
- How would you deploy or run it outside the lab?

**Data, testing and results**
- How did you test it? What was the hardest bug and how did you fix it?
- What results did you get, and how do you know they are correct?
- Why is the result not better? What limits it?

**Novelty and comparison**
- What already exists? How is yours different?
- Why not use an existing tool?

**Contribution and learning**
- What exactly did you do, personally?
- What would you change if you started again?
- What would the next version include?

Add questions from your own branch. A mechanical project will be asked about design choices, materials, loads or tolerances. A civil project will be asked about assumptions and codes. An electronics project will be asked about components and circuit behaviour. A software project will be asked about data and security. Ask your guide what they usually ask.

## Step 4: Practise your demo and your backup plan

- Keep the demo short. A 3 to 5 minute walk-through of the main flow is easier to control than a long one. Check your time limit with your department.
- Start from the entry point and show the most important feature first.
- Test it on the machine you will use, with the network you will have.
- Prepare a backup: screenshots, a short screen recording, or a printed circuit or design diagram, in case something fails.

## Step 5: Rehearse out loud

Reading your notes silently is not practice. Explain your project to a friend, a junior or a mentor, and ask them to interrupt with "why?" and "how do you know?". Record yourself once. If you cannot explain a diagram in your own words, you do not know it well enough yet.

## A 7-day preparation plan

| Day | Focus |
|---|---|
| 1 | Confirm rules with your guide. Write the one-page project map. |
| 2 | Re-read your full report. Fix any mismatch with the build. |
| 3 | Revise the technical basics behind your project (for example, your main algorithm, circuit or design method). |
| 4 | Write short answers to the question themes in Step 3. |
| 5 | Practise the demo and prepare the backup. |
| 6 | Hold a mock viva with a friend or mentor. Note weak answers. |
| 7 | Light revision. Pack your documents. Sleep early. |

If you have fewer than seven days, do days 1, 2, 4 and 6 first.

## What to do when you do not know the answer

Do not guess with confidence. Say what you do understand, explain how you would find out, and connect it to your project. A calm, honest answer shows reasoning, which examiners usually value more than a wrong answer delivered boldly.

A simple four-part answer works for most questions: **answer directly, give project context, give evidence, state the limit.**

## Mistakes to avoid

- Memorising answers without understanding them.
- Reading from your slides.
- Not knowing your own teammate's module in a team project. Examiners may ask about the whole project.
- A report that does not match the working build.
- Claiming results you cannot show or repeat.
- Using code, text or ideas you cannot explain. If you used AI tools or online sources, follow your college's rules and be ready to explain every part.
- Untested demo, with no backup.

## FAQ

### What is asked in a final year project viva?
Examiners usually ask about the problem you solved, your design choices, the tools you used, how you tested the work, your results, what is new about it, and what you personally did. Expect follow-up questions on any diagram, line of code or claim in your report.

### How do I prepare for a project viva in one week?
Confirm the rules on day 1, write a one-page project map, check that your report matches your build, prepare short answers to common questions, practise your demo with a backup, and hold a mock viva before the day. See the 7-day plan above.

### What if I do not know the answer in a viva?
Say what you do know, explain how you would check the rest, and link it to your project. Examiners usually value clear reasoning more than a confident guess.

### Do I need to know my teammates' parts in a group project?
Yes, know at least the overview of every module. Examiners may ask anyone about any part of the project, as well as about your own contribution.

### Should I memorise answers for the viva?
No. Memorised answers break down when the examiner changes the question. Understand your project well enough to explain it in your own words.

### How long should my project demo be?
Ask your department for the time limit. If you are free to choose, a short walk-through of the main flow, around 3 to 5 minutes, is easier to control. Always prepare a backup in case the demo fails.
